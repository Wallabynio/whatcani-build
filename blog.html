DA vs CDC: which path suits your project?

9 Jul 2026 · 10 min read

The core distinction is this: a Complying Development Certificate (CDC) is a fast-tracked, rules-based approval for standard, low-impact projects, while a Development Application (DA) is a slower, merit-based assessment that buys you more design flexibility and discretion from council planners. Choosing between them is primarily a feasibility decision about how tightly your design can fit the code, how much risk you can tolerate, and how much control you need over the outcome.

1. What DA and CDC actually are

A Development Application in NSW is a formal request for development consent under the Environmental Planning and Assessment Act 1979, assessed by your local council against State Environmental Planning Policies (SEPPs), the Local Environmental Plan (LEP) and relevant Development Control Plans (DCPs). It is merit-based: planners weigh impacts (bulk and scale, overshadowing, character, traffic, heritage, amenity) and can exercise discretion, request changes, or refuse the application outright.

A Complying Development Certificate is a combined planning and construction approval for straightforward development that meets pre-set numerical standards in State codes, most commonly the Housing and other Codes SEPP. If the proposal and site meet every listed control (height, setbacks, private open space, landscaping, car parking, etc.), an accredited certifier or council must issue the CDC within statutory time frames; if it fails any control, the application simply cannot proceed as complying development.

Under current NSW settings, all DA and CDC applications must be lodged through the NSW Planning Portal, with defined document sets (plans, reports, certificates) and online payment of fees. This means both pathways now share a digitised process, but they diverge sharply in how they treat discretion, public participation, and time frames.

2. Timeframes: speed vs certainty

CDC approvals are typically issued within 10–20 business days for standard projects, and as little as 10 days under the NSW Housing Pattern Book complying development pathway, provided the proposal is fully compliant and the documentation is complete. This accelerated turn-around is a deliberate policy choice: complying development is meant to clear low-impact, standardised projects quickly, freeing councils from routine assessments.

DA assessments routinely take months rather than weeks, with published ranges of roughly 3–12 months depending on council resourcing, complexity, neighbour objections and the need for redesign. The process includes notification periods, possible referrals to design review panels or heritage advisers, and iterative negotiations over changes, all of which extend the timeline and can introduce significant uncertainty.

In practice, the time advantage of a CDC evaporates if your design only marginally fits the codes and needs repeated redesign to achieve strict compliance. Conversely, although DAs are slower on paper, a well-prepared DA that aligns with council controls and engages stakeholders early can avoid major delays, particularly in councils with established processes for pre-lodgement meetings and design review.

3. Risk profile: approval risk vs design risk

Risk under a CDC pathway is primarily binary: if your project and site satisfy every relevant standard, approval must be granted; if not, it cannot be approved as complying development at all. The approval risk is lower once compliance is proven, but the design risk is higher upfront because even minor deviations (for example, a setback short by a few hundred millimetres) can force a complete change of pathway.

By contrast, DA outcomes sit on a spectrum: council planners can approve, approve with conditions, or refuse, and they may accept modest variations from numeric standards where the overall impact is acceptable. This introduces higher approval risk in the sense that outcomes are less predictable, but lower design risk in that you can argue for performance-based solutions rather than strict rule adherence.

Neighbour and community risk also diverge. CDCs generally do not require formal neighbour notification or a public exhibition period, which limits third-party input and objection risk but can create political and reputational sensitivities where projects are perceived as “bypassing council”. DAs require public notification and allow submissions; objections can delay assessment, trigger additional conditions, or even lead to refusal, particularly in politically sensitive or character-focused neighbourhoods.

For complex or contentious projects, some practitioners treat the DA process as a managed risk environment: you accept the possibility of objection and delay in exchange for the chance to negotiate outcomes, adjust the design, and demonstrate merit. For standard low-impact work in benign contexts, a CDC often represents a lower-risk pathway provided feasibility work confirms compliance at the outset.

4. Design flexibility and project typology

CDC standards are tight and prescriptive. They specify maximum heights, setbacks, site coverage, private open space, car parks, and sometimes materiality or façade treatments, with little scope to deviate without leaving the complying development framework. For straightforward detached dwellings, secondary dwellings, simple extensions, and some duplex or small commercial projects, these standards can be met with relatively conventional designs.

DA assessment, being merit-based, allows more freedom to pursue non-standard forms, complex site responses (steep slopes, irregular lots), and context-specific design moves that might not sit neatly within CDC tables. Heritage-listed properties, conservation areas, mixed-use developments, and ambitious architectural projects almost always require DA because the planning system expects case-by-case evaluation of their impacts and contributions.

Recent NSW policy proposals around “CDC variations” aim to allow minor deviations from strict complying development standards while retaining a fast-track character, but these mechanisms are still emerging and details are subject to change. Until they are fully implemented, the working assumption should be that CDCs are effectively zero-tolerance on standards, and anything non-standard belongs in a DA.

For production-heavy or design-intensive projects (for example, high-concept dwellings or small studios where spatial and aesthetic choices are central), the DA pathway typically aligns better with the need to negotiate unusual solutions with planners. For more utilitarian builds where speed and cost control dominate over formal innovation, CDC is closer to a code-compliant checklist that trades flexibility for predictability.

5. Practical feasibility: how to decide DA vs CDC

NSW practice increasingly treats DA vs CDC as a feasibility question answered early, before detailed design fees are committed. Typical feasibility steps include: checking zoning and overlays, reviewing the applicable SEPP codes and LEP/DCP controls, mapping site constraints (slope, flooding, heritage, easements), and sketching test layouts against key CDC standards such as setbacks, private open space, and solar access.

Pre-lodgement meetings with council planners are recommended for both pathways but are especially useful if you anticipate needing a DA; they allow you to test appetite for variations and to identify likely conditions or concerns before committing to a design. For CDC, feasibility often involves liaison with an accredited certifier early on, to confirm the site and concept are eligible under the chosen code.

Documentation demands are similar at a baseline level: both pathways require site plans, floor plans, elevations, sections, BASIX certificates for residential projects, and design verification statements confirming code compliance. DA typically adds impact assessments (Statement of Environmental Effects, traffic reports, heritage reports, view or overshadowing studies), whereas CDC focuses on evidence that each relevant numeric control is satisfied.

Costs are highly project-specific, but available industry guidance suggests that CDC approvals have lower and more predictable statutory costs due to shorter time frames and simpler documentation, whereas DAs tend to incur higher council fees and professional costs over longer assessment periods. This distinction is material in feasibility studies, but should be weighed against the potential for expensive redesign if you attempt a marginal CDC and later discover it does not comply.

7. Critical limitations and uncertainties

Most publicly available commentary on DA vs CDC in NSW comes from consultants, designers, builders or certifiers with commercial interests in particular pathways, which introduces bias in favour of whichever process they specialise in or can deliver fastest. These sources rarely provide systematic empirical data on approval times, refusal rates or cost differentials, and instead rely on indicative ranges and anecdotal experience, which limits the statistical robustness of their claims.

The regulatory framework is also volatile. NSW has recently proposed new “complying development variations” pathways to allow minor deviations from CDC standards, but these are subject to ongoing consultation and may change materially before full implementation. Relying on early descriptions of such mechanisms for strategic decision-making is therefore risky; project teams should treat them as provisional until the final instruments are gazetted.

Finally, while rules-based CDC pathways appear more objective, their strictness means small errors in interpretation or documentation can have disproportionate consequences, and guidance on how certifiers will treat borderline cases is limited. DA processes, despite their flexibility, suffer from inconsistency between councils and over time, and there is little publicly accessible, peer-reviewed research quantifying this variation across NSW, which leaves practitioners reliant on local experience rather than generalisable evidence.

What zoning R2 actually means for your site

23 Jul 2026 · 9 min read

R2 Low Density Residential is the most common residential zone in Sydney, and the one most often misread. The zone label tells you which uses are on the menu; it does not tell you what you can build. The numbers that decide that — minimum lot size, height, floor space ratio, setbacks, landscaped area — sit in separate clauses of your Local Environmental Plan, in the Development Control Plan behind it, and increasingly in a State policy that overrides both. Two sites in the same R2 zone, a few streets apart, can carry very different development.

1. Where the R2 rules actually come from

R2 is not a council invention. It is one of the standard residential zones in the Standard Instrument (Local Environmental Plans) Order 2006, the template every council in NSW must use when it writes its LEP. That is why the zone name, the zone objectives and the broad shape of the land use table look familiar from one council to the next: they are drawn from a single State template rather than written locally.

The standard objectives of the R2 zone are to provide for the housing needs of the community within a low density residential environment, and to enable other land uses that provide facilities or services to meet the day to day needs of residents. Those objectives matter more than they appear. When a proposal sits at the edge of a control, an assessing planner will test it against the objectives of the zone, and a scheme that plainly reads as medium density in a low density street will struggle regardless of how the numbers stack up.

Beneath the objectives sits the land use table, which sorts every use into three buckets: permitted without consent, permitted with consent, and prohibited. Councils adapt the table within limits, so R2 in Ryde is not identical to R2 in Sutherland Shire. The zone gets you to the right chapter; only the specific LEP tells you the rules.

2. What you can build in R2

The core of the R2 land use table is a dwelling house, a secondary dwelling (a granny flat), a dual occupancy and semi-detached dwellings, all with development consent. Around that core, most councils also permit a longer list with consent: boarding houses, child care centres, community facilities, educational establishments, group homes, health consulting rooms, home businesses and home industries, neighbourhood shops, respite day care centres and, in some LEPs, shop top housing. The exact list is set by your council’s land use table, and the differences between councils are real.

Two State reforms have widened that core materially. From 1 July 2024, dual occupancies and semi-detached dwellings became permissible with consent across R2 zones throughout NSW, removing the patchwork where some councils allowed duplexes and neighbouring councils did not. From 28 February 2025, the second stage of the Low and Mid-Rise Housing Policy went further, permitting terraces, manor houses and multi dwelling housing, and in places low-rise residential flat buildings, on R2 land within roughly 800 metres walking distance of a list of nominated town centres and transport hubs.

That second reform is geographically targeted rather than universal. Whether your R2 site sits inside or outside one of those walking catchments is often the single largest determinant of what it is worth, and it is not something the zone colour on a map will tell you.

3. The controls that actually decide yield

Permissibility is the beginning of the analysis, not the end. In practice the binding constraints are numerical, and they live outside the land use table:

Minimum lot size. Clause 4.1 of the LEP sets the minimum lot size for subdivision, commonly 450 square metres in R2 but ranging widely by council and by locality. It is the control that most often decides whether a dual occupancy can be subdivided into two saleable lots, which is usually where the margin sits.

Height of buildings. Mapped under clause 4.3, typically 8.5 or 9.5 metres in R2. The difference between those two figures decides whether a genuine two-storey scheme with a pitched roof works on a sloping site.

Floor space ratio. Mapped under clause 4.4 where it applies. Not every R2 area has a mapped FSR, and where it does not, bulk is governed by height, setbacks and landscaped area instead.

The DCP layer. Setbacks, landscaped area, deep soil, private open space, solar access, driveway widths, parking and tree retention are usually in the Development Control Plan rather than the LEP. A DCP is not law in the same way an LEP is, but a scheme that ignores it will attract conditions, objections and delay.

Under the Stage 2 reforms, dual occupancy on qualifying R2 land is subject to non-discretionary development standards, including a minimum lot size of 450 square metres, a minimum lot width of 12 metres, a maximum floor space ratio of 0.65:1 and a maximum height of 9.5 metres, with subdivision permitted down to 225 square metres per lot at a minimum 6 metre width. These State standards override the equivalent LEP and DCP provisions, except where the local control is more generous.

4. What R2 prohibits, and where projects come unstuck

Outside the mapped Stage 2 catchments, residential flat buildings remain prohibited in R2, as do most commercial, retail beyond a neighbourhood shop, and industrial uses. Prohibition is absolute: there is no variation clause that makes a prohibited use permissible, and clause 4.6 cannot be used to overcome it. This is a common and expensive misunderstanding — clause 4.6 varies a development standard such as height or floor space ratio, not the permissibility of a use.

The more frequent problem, though, is not the zone at all. It is the overlays sitting on top of it. Heritage conservation areas, bushfire prone land, flood planning areas, acid sulfate soils, biodiversity values land, and easements or rights of carriageway do not change what the zone permits, but any one of them can stop a scheme or reshape it entirely. A site can be perfectly zoned for a duplex and still be undevelopable in the form the buyer had in mind. Checking the zone without checking the overlays is the single most common failure we see in pre-purchase due diligence.

5. Practical implications for a feasibility

A defensible R2 feasibility works through four questions in order. Is the use permissible under this council’s land use table, and does the Housing SEPP widen that? Does the site meet the numeric thresholds for the form you want, particularly minimum lot size and width? What do the overlays and site constraints do to the developable envelope once setbacks, landscaped area and deep soil are applied? And finally, what is this council actually approving on comparable sites, which is a question the controls alone will not answer.

Yield should be derived from that sequence, not assumed from the zone. The gap between a theoretical yield calculated off site area and a realistic yield after controls and constraints are applied is routinely one dwelling, and one dwelling is usually the difference between a project that works and one that does not.

7. Critical limitations and uncertainties

Much of the accessible commentary on R2 zoning and the low and mid-rise reforms is published by designers, builders, certifiers and agents with a commercial interest in a particular outcome, and it tends to describe the most favourable reading of the rules rather than the assessed reality. Very little of it is accompanied by data on approval rates, refusals or the conditions councils are imposing under the new standards.

The policy settings are also still moving. The Low and Mid-Rise Housing Policy has commenced in stages, its mapped catchments and non-discretionary standards have been refined since first exhibition, and councils have applied them inconsistently while their own instruments catch up. Where a State standard and a local control conflict, the resolution is not always obvious on the face of the documents, and practice is still forming.

Finally, none of this substitutes for the instruments themselves. The only reliable answer for a specific property comes from that council’s LEP and DCP as currently in force, the mapping on the NSW Planning Portal, the title and any restrictions on it, and where the question is finely balanced, a pre-lodgement discussion with the council. Zone labels are a starting point for a conversation, not a development yield.

5 things to check before buying a development site

28 Sep 2026 · 8 min read

Most development sites that fail do so because of information that was available before purchase. The zoning looked right, the lot looked big enough, and the numbers worked on a spreadsheet. Then, after exchange, a flood notation, an easement, a heritage listing or a sewer main running under the only buildable part of the lot changes the answer. None of those things are hidden. They sit in documents a buyer can read, or order, in the few days before contracts are exchanged. Here are the five checks every buyer should run in that window, and what each one can tell you.

1. The planning controls: what the site can legally carry

Start with the rules that decide what can be built, not with the listing agent’s description of what “could” be built. The zone in the council’s Local Environmental Plan (LEP) tells you which uses are permissible. The numbers that decide yield sit in separate clauses: minimum lot size for subdivision (clause 4.1), height of buildings (clause 4.3) and floor space ratio (clause 4.4), where one is mapped. Setbacks, landscaped area, deep soil, parking and private open space sit in the council’s Development Control Plan (DCP).

On top of the local instruments, State policy now does a lot of the work. The Housing SEPP has made dual occupancies permissible with consent across R2 zones, and its low and mid-rise provisions allow terraces, townhouses and, in places, small apartment buildings within walking distance of nominated town centres and stations. Whether a site sits inside one of those catchments can matter more than anything in the LEP.

The vendor must attach a planning certificate under section 10.7(2) of the Environmental Planning and Assessment Act to the contract for sale. It lists the zone, the instruments that apply, whether complying development can be carried out on the land, and hazards such as flooding and bushfire. It is worth ordering the fuller section 10.7(5) version as well, which adds advice the council holds on matters such as contamination or local policies. Read the certificate line by line; it is the single most useful document in the contract.

2. Hazards and overlays: what can stop or reshape a scheme

Overlays do not change what the zone permits, but any one of them can stop a scheme or reshape it entirely. Three do most of the damage.

Heritage. A heritage item, or a site inside a heritage conservation area, usually rules out complying development for new dwellings. The Low Rise Housing Diversity Code, the fast-track route for many duplexes and terraces, cannot be used on heritage items or in heritage conservation areas. That leaves a development application (DA), assessed on merit against the character of the area, with longer timeframes and less certainty about the form a council will accept.

Flooding. Since the State’s flood-prone land package took effect in July 2021, every LEP carries a standard flood clause, and flood-related controls are noted on the planning certificate. Land in the flood planning area usually needs habitable floors raised above the flood planning level, which changes building height, access, cost and sometimes the number of dwellings a site can carry. Some flood-affected lots are also excluded from complying development.

Bushfire. On bush fire prone land, development must meet Planning for Bush Fire Protection 2019, and the site’s bushfire attack level (BAL) drives construction standards and cost. Complying development is only available at the lower levels, BAL-LOW to BAL-29; sites rated BAL-40 or Flame Zone have to go through a DA.

Also look for acid sulfate soils, biodiversity mapping, land reserved for a public purpose such as a road widening, and any history that suggests contamination. Most of these appear on the planning certificate and the NSW Planning Portal’s spatial viewer.

3. The title: what others already have a right to

The title search attached to the contract shows who owns the land and what is registered against it. What matters for development is often in the fine print: easements for drainage or services, rights of carriageway, restrictions on the use of land and positive covenants. Many of these are created by a section 88B instrument registered with the deposited plan, and the instrument is not always attached to the contract. Order it. A restriction limiting the lot to one dwelling, or a drainage easement across the middle of the site, can end a feasibility on its own.

Check the sewer service diagram too, which is attached to the contract. Sydney Water requires approval before anyone builds over or next to its pipes, and a main running through the lot can push a building away from where the feasibility assumed it would sit, or add the cost of an engineering solution. A sewer or stormwater asset in the wrong place is one of the most common reasons a second dwelling will not fit.

4. The physical site: what the land itself allows

Controls describe an envelope; the land decides how much of it is usable. Four physical checks catch most of the surprises.

Width, depth and shape. Minimum lot width matters as much as area. Within the State’s low and mid-rise housing areas, a dual occupancy needs a lot at least 12 metres wide; the complying development code generally asks for 15 metres at the building line. Battle-axe lots are excluded from the Low Rise Housing Diversity Code, so a rear lot usually means a DA.

Slope and stormwater. A site that falls away from the street may need an easement through a downhill neighbour’s land to drain stormwater. If the neighbour will not grant one, the options are expensive on-site solutions or a smaller scheme.

Trees. Clearing vegetation on urban land is controlled by the State’s Biodiversity and Conservation SEPP and by the council’s own DCP, which sets which trees need a permit. A significant tree, on the site or close to its boundary, can take a large bite out of the building footprint once its root zone is protected.

Access and services. Street trees, power poles, substations, bus stops and driveway crossings on the frontage all limit where a second driveway can go, and a second driveway is often what a duplex needs.

5. The numbers: what the project will actually cost and return

The last check brings the first four together. Work out a realistic yield, not a theoretical one: what the controls allow once setbacks, landscaped area, deep soil, overlays and site constraints are applied. The gap between those two figures is routinely one dwelling, and one dwelling is usually the margin.

Then add the costs a purchase price does not show. Local infrastructure contributions under section 7.11 or 7.12 are set by each council’s contributions plan. The State’s Housing and Productivity Contribution, in place since October 2023, adds roughly $10,000 per additional dwelling in Greater Sydney, indexed each year. Subdivision needs a Section 73 compliance certificate from Sydney Water, with its own charges and timing. Holding costs, approval timeframes and construction cost escalation all belong in the model before exchange, not after.

Finally, look at what the council is actually approving. The NSW Planning Portal and council application trackers show recent determinations on nearby sites. A scheme that complies on paper but has no precedent in the street carries more risk than one that follows a pattern the council has already approved.

7. Critical limitations and uncertainties

A planning certificate records what the council knows at the date it is issued. It is not a survey, it does not assess a particular scheme, and a clear certificate is not a guarantee that a proposal will be approved. The fuller section 10.7(5) advice is only as complete as the council’s records.

The rules are also moving. The low and mid-rise housing provisions have commenced in stages and have been refined since they were first exhibited, the Housing and Productivity Contribution is indexed and has been extended, and flood guidance continues to be updated. Figures quoted here, including the contribution rate, will change; the current instrument or fee schedule is the only reliable source on the day.

Much of the accessible commentary on pre-purchase checks is written by conveyancers, certifiers, designers and search providers with a commercial interest in a particular service, and it tends to present the most favourable or the most cautious reading. None of this replaces advice on the contract itself from a solicitor or conveyancer, and where a finding is finely balanced, a conversation with the council before exchange.

Policy updates

Housing SEPP changes: what they mean for duplexes

28 Sep 2026 · 7 min read

The Housing SEPP has been amended so often since it commenced in late 2021 that much of what is written about duplexes online is already out of date. For anyone weighing up a dual occupancy in NSW, three changes matter most: duplexes became permissible in every R2 zone in July 2024, a set of State standards now overrides council controls in the low and mid-rise housing areas from February 2025, and a fast-track complying development pathway for pre-designed homes opened in July 2025. Here is where each one stands, what it changed, and what it did not.

1. What the Housing SEPP is

State Environmental Planning Policy (Housing) 2021 brought several older housing policies, including those for affordable rental housing and seniors housing, into a single instrument. It sits above every council’s Local Environmental Plan (LEP): where the two conflict, the State policy generally prevails. That is why a change to the Housing SEPP can alter what is possible on a site overnight without the council changing a word of its own plan.

For duplexes, the SEPP now does two separate jobs. It decides where a dual occupancy is permissible, and in some areas it sets the numbers the design has to meet. The approval pathways, a development application (DA) or a complying development certificate (CDC), sit alongside it in other instruments.

2. July 2024: duplexes permitted in every R2 zone

The first stage of the Low and Mid-Rise Housing Policy started on 1 July 2024. It made dual occupancies and semi-detached dwellings permissible with consent in the R2 Low Density Residential zone across all of NSW. Before that, a handful of councils prohibited duplexes in R2 altogether, so neighbouring streets on either side of a council boundary could carry very different potential.

What Stage 1 did not change matters just as much. Permissibility is not yield. The council’s minimum lot size for dual occupancies, its height and floor space controls, and the setbacks, landscaping and parking rules in its Development Control Plan (DCP) all still apply. A site that was too small or too narrow for a duplex before July 2024 is usually still too small or too narrow today.

3. February 2025: State standards in the low and mid-rise housing areas

The second stage started on 28 February 2025. It applies to residential land within about 800 metres walking distance of a nominated town centre or train, metro or light rail station, in Greater Sydney, the Central Coast, the Lower Hunter and Newcastle, and Illawarra-Shoalhaven. The Bathurst, Hawkesbury, Blue Mountains and Wollondilly council areas are excluded because of bushfire, flood and evacuation risk, and the policy does not apply to sites affected by certain hazards, such as bushfire prone land and some flood prone land.

Inside those areas, a dual occupancy is subject to non-discretionary development standards:

Lot size and width. A minimum lot size of 450 square metres and a minimum width of 12 metres.

Floor space and height. A maximum floor space ratio of 0.65:1 and a maximum height of 9.5 metres.

Parking. One car space per dwelling.

Subdivision. In the R1, R2 and R3 zones, the two dwellings can be subdivided down to 225 square metres per lot, with a minimum width of 6 metres.

“Non-discretionary” has a specific meaning. Where a proposal meets one of these standards, the council cannot require more on that matter, and the standard prevails over a more onerous equivalent in the LEP or DCP. It does not guarantee approval. Every other matter, from privacy and overshadowing to stormwater and trees, is still assessed on merit.

4. The fast-track pathways

A duplex can be approved in one of three ways, and the Housing SEPP changes only affect which ones are open on a given site.

Development application. Assessed by the council on merit against the LEP, the DCP and the Housing SEPP. Slower, but flexible: it is the only route on heritage-listed land, in heritage conservation areas and on most constrained sites.

Complying development under the Low Rise Housing Diversity Code. Issued by a council or a registered certifier where the design meets fixed standards. The lot must meet the council’s minimum lot size for dual occupancies, or 400 square metres where the LEP sets none, and generally be at least 15 metres wide at the building line (12 metres where vehicle access is from a secondary road or lane). The code cannot be used on heritage items, in heritage conservation areas, on battle-axe lots or on unsewered land.

The Pattern Book pathway. Since July 2025, a set of pre-designed homes from the NSW Housing Pattern Book, including dual occupancies, can be approved as complying development in the R1, R2 and R3 zones where the housing type is permitted. A certifier must determine a pattern book application within 10 days of receiving all the required information. Bushfire prone land, flood control lots, battle-axe lots, unsewered land and heritage land are excluded, and the design has to be built as drawn, with only limited adjustments.

5. What it means for a duplex site today

A sound duplex assessment now works through five questions in order. Is a dual occupancy permitted? In R2, almost always yes since July 2024, but check the zone. Is the site inside a low and mid-rise housing area? If so, the State standards apply and usually help. Does the lot meet the minimum size and width for the pathway you want? That is where most sites fall out. Which approval pathway is realistically open, given heritage, bushfire, flood and lot shape? And can the result be subdivided onto separate titles, which is usually where the margin sits?

Costs have moved as well. A duplex in Greater Sydney now attracts the State’s Housing and Productivity Contribution on the additional dwelling, roughly $10,000 and indexed each year, on top of the council’s local contributions.

7. Critical limitations and uncertainties

The settings are still moving. The low and mid-rise provisions have been refined since they were first exhibited, the Pattern Book has had designs added since launch, and councils are still bringing their own plans into line with the new standards. Figures and dates here reflect the published policy at the time of writing; the instrument in force on the day is the only reliable source.

Much of the commentary on the reforms comes from designers, builders, certifiers and buyers’ agents with an interest in more duplexes being built, and it tends to describe the most favourable reading. Early approval data under the new standards is thin, and there is little public evidence yet on how often non-discretionary standards are carrying proposals through council assessment.

Finally, the State standards cover only a handful of matters. Whether a particular duplex works on a particular lot still depends on the council’s LEP and DCP, the site’s hazards and title, and a design that responds to both. The reforms have widened what is possible; they have not removed the need to test each site.

Case study

Townhouse feasibility: inner west site, 2025

28 Sep 2026 · 7 min read

In 2025 an owner-developer came to us three weeks from exchange on a corner site of about 650 m² in Earlwood, in the Canterbury-Bankstown council area. The land was zoned R3 Medium Density Residential, and the plan was four townhouses. The question was simple: would the controls carry four? Six days later they had the answer: realistically three, with a development application (DA) risk profile their lender could work with. This is a walkthrough of how that review ran, what the controls allowed, and why the number changed. The client’s name is withheld and identifying details have been changed; their financial figures stay confidential.

1. The brief

The client had a contract in hand, a lender waiting for a feasibility, and a yield of four in their head. Four is a common assumption on a site of this size in a medium density zone: divide the land by the number of dwellings, allow for a driveway, and it looks like it fits. What that sum leaves out is the development control plan (DCP), the council’s detailed rulebook for setbacks, open space and building form. On a corner block the DCP bites harder than most people expect.

The brief was therefore narrow and time-bound: before exchange, confirm what the site could realistically yield, and identify anything that would make a DA risky enough to worry a lender.

2. Permissibility: the first gate

The starting point is the Canterbury-Bankstown Local Environmental Plan (LEP) 2023, the council’s legal planning instrument. It lists multi dwelling housing, the planning term for townhouses and villas, as permitted with consent in the R3 zone. “With consent” means a DA is needed; the use is allowed, but the design still has to be approved.

Permissibility is only the first gate. The LEP also sets a larger minimum lot size for multi dwelling housing in some mapped parts of the city, and the DCP asks for a minimum street frontage of 20 metres for this kind of development on a local road. Both need checking before anything else, because failing either can end a townhouse scheme outright. On this site both hurdles were cleared, so the real question was yield, not whether townhouses were allowed at all.

3. The corner-site envelope

The building envelope is the three-dimensional space a design can occupy once every setback and height limit is applied. For multi dwelling housing in the former Canterbury area, Chapter 5.2 of the Canterbury-Bankstown DCP 2023 sets controls that shape that envelope quickly:

Front setback: at least 6 metres from the front boundary on a local road in the R3 zone (9 metres on a major road).

Corner lots: at least 5.5 metres from the longer street frontage as well. On a corner, two sides of the site are street frontages, so both carry a deep setback.

Side setbacks: 1.5 metres for dwellings facing the street and 2.5 metres for buildings behind them.

Rear setback: at least 3 metres where the building is single storey, with deep-soil planting zones of 3 or 5 metres along the front and rear boundaries.

Height in storeys: two storeys at most, dropping to one storey for any part of a building more than 20 metres back from the street, or beyond 65% of the lot’s length.

Each control on its own looks modest. Together, on a corner, they remove a large share of the land from the building footprint, and the single-storey rule means the dwellings at the back of the site cannot simply go up to recover the space.

4. Testing four against three

We tested the envelope against the DCP setbacks rather than starting from a floor plan. That order matters: a floor plan drawn first tends to find a way to fit the number the client wants, while an envelope drawn first shows what the rules leave room for.

The open-space controls add to the squeeze. The DCP asks for 40 m² of private open space for each dwelling, including an area of at least 2.5 by 2.5 metres for outdoor dining, and ground-level open space needs to be at least 4 metres wide in every direction. Four dwellings need 160 m² of usable private yards; three need 120 m². On a lot of about 650 m² that difference is significant, because the yards have to sit inside the space left after the setbacks, the deep-soil zones and a shared driveway.

With four dwellings, the scheme only worked by pushing against several controls at once. That would have meant asking council to accept variations to the DCP setbacks, with the yards squeezed as a result, and each variation is a point where a DA can be refused, delayed or cut back. With three, the design sat comfortably inside the envelope. Our advice was that three was the realistic yield: four was not impossible to lodge, but it was not something a buyer should pay for.

5. A DA risk profile a lender can use

Lenders funding a development site want to know two things about the approval: how likely it is, and what could delay it. A feasibility that only reports the best case does not answer either. Alongside the yield, we set out a short risk profile for the three-dwelling scheme: which controls it met outright, where assessment judgement would still apply, and what could slow the DA, such as neighbour objections or requests for more information.

The value of that document was that it was honest in both directions. It did not promise an approval, and it did not bury the client in caveats. It gave the lender a clear, compliant scheme to assess instead of an optimistic one.

6. What changed for the client

The advice landed before exchange, which is the only point at which it could change the outcome. With a realistic yield of three rather than four, the client could test the price against a number the controls supported, and go into the purchase with a scheme their lender could back. The time cost was six days; the alternative was discovering the same answer after settlement, with a DA already drawn up for four.

Three lessons carry over to most townhouse sites:

Yield is set by the DCP, not the lot area. Dividing the land by a target dwelling size is a starting guess, not a feasibility.

Corner sites are not automatically better. Two street frontages mean two deep setbacks, and the gain in access is often smaller than the loss in footprint.

Test before exchange. A few days of work before the contract is signed is worth more than months of redesign afterwards.

8. Critical limitations and uncertainties

This is a single engagement, anonymised, and it should be read as an example of a method rather than a rule about Earlwood or about corner sites. Every lot differs in shape, slope, orientation and neighbours, and a site of the same area elsewhere could yield more or less.

The DCP figures quoted are those in the currently published Chapter 5.2, which has been amended since the review took place. Councils update their DCPs regularly, and some controls apply differently to particular streets, heritage items or major roads. The instrument in force on the day of lodgement is the only reliable source.

State policy can also override council controls. The NSW low and mid-rise housing reforms and the Transport Oriented Development program apply to land near some stations and centres in the Canterbury-Bankstown area, including around Turrella, where the walking catchment reaches into parts of Earlwood. Where those provisions apply, different standards and pathways may produce a different answer from the one reached here. Finally, planning feasibility is only one input: construction costs, sale prices and finance terms decide whether a scheme is worth building, and those figures are not reported here.
